\newcommand{\PaperTitle}{Risk-Aware Scheduling for Emergency Building Sweeps}
\newcommand{\SummaryText}{%
Emergency building sweeps require coordinated room inspections, responder assignments, and verification of high-risk areas. We seek to reduce total clearance time while maintaining full room coverage and the required verification procedures. The model combines \textbf{building topology, evolving hazards, and task scheduling} to evaluate sweep strategies across building layouts and emergency conditions.

\textbf{For Problem 2,} we plan a sweep of a single-story, six-room building by two responders. We develop a \textbf{task allocation and route planning model based on a dynamic weighted graph} to minimize the latest responder completion time. Rooms, corridors, and exits form the graph, with travel costs updated to reflect smoke concentrations and responder fatigue; priorities combine intrinsic room risk with current smoke concentrations. A \textbf{risk-penalized Dijkstra algorithm and a priority-based greedy heuristic} assign high-risk rooms first, followed by verification by a different responder. In a scenario containing two high-risk laboratories, responders start at opposite exits and complete all inspections in \textbf{124.6~s}. The plan achieves \textbf{full primary coverage and verification of both laboratories}, with individual completion times of 114.6 and 124.6~s and a 15~s recheck of each laboratory.

\textbf{For Problem 3,} we examine how building height, room use, and staffing affect sweep schedules. We develop a \textbf{multi-floor coordinated sweep scheduling model} by extending the baseline formulation. The model incorporates stair travel at half the horizontal movement speed, vertical smoke attenuation, and floor priorities, with verification requirements for daycare and chemical storage areas. A three-floor building with \textbf{12 rooms and four responders} requires \textbf{145.7~s}; a five-floor building with \textbf{18 rooms and six responders} requires \textbf{238.8~s}. The daycare receives its primary inspection by approximately 41.9~s; both scenarios complete all required high-risk verification tasks. In the three-floor case, the coefficient of variation of responder completion times reaches \textbf{24.5\%}, exceeding the 15\% target and indicating that verification timing can produce uneven workloads.

\textbf{For Problem 4,} we assess communication degradation, occupant congestion, and real-time sensing. We develop a \textbf{dynamic scheduling model with communication and congestion constraints}. Communication reliability decays over time, and corridor occupancy changes travel speeds. Time-dependent fire and gas hazards also change room priorities, allowing the schedule to respond to evolving local conditions. The model switches to autonomous zone assignments when reliability drops below \textbf{70\%} and uses sensor data to update risk information. Under the simulated conditions, sensors reduce completion times for the single-story, three-floor, and five-floor scenarios to \textbf{103.2, 118.9, and 187.6~s}, respectively, corresponding to reductions of \textbf{17.2\%, 18.4\%, and 21.4\%}.

Across \textbf{10 runs per strategy}, random assignment, nearest-neighbor routing, and equal partitioning yield mean completion times of 205.4, 222.0, and 209.6~s, respectively, all exceeding the proposed method's 124.6~s. Sensitivity analysis examines perturbations of \textbf{$\pm20\%$ in smoke diffusion parameters} and \textbf{$\pm15\%$ in movement speed}. Results assume uniform responder capabilities, intact building routes, and manually specified risk weights; practical use requires further calibration against building data and evacuation drills.
